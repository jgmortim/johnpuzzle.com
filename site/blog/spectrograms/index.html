0 // Overview

A spectrogram is a visual representation of the spectrum of frequencies of a signal over time. Spectrograms, especially in audio signals, are an extremely common technique used in ARGs and puzzle hunts. They are so common that, on their own, they present virtually no challenge to those with a modicum of ARG experience. This article serve as a case study of my exploration of the medium of spectrograms and dives into ways to increase variety and difficulty in spectrogram-based puzzles.

1 // Barcode

In Puzzle 10 of the Find Buzzy ARG, I designed a .wav file for which the spectrogram displayed a standard Code-128 barcode. And scanning the barcode revealed the answer to the puzzle. This is a simple technique, but it gets you out of the cliché of simple text. Viewing the spectrogram is now just one step in decoding the file.

2 // Track Manipulation

2.1 // First Iteration

In Puzzle 12 of the 2025 XIXMAS ARG, I wanted to create an audio file that had to be manipulated before the spectrogram would reveal its message. What I came up with was a stereo track called Two Voices, One Truth, which is hosted on SoundCloud. For context, part of the puzzle was first finding the SoundCloud account. The track appears to be just white noise and the album art is also TV static. However, if you mix the stereo down to mono (as hinted by the track name), all the noise cancels out and the true message is revealed. In Audacity, this can be done with: Tracks > Mix > Mix Stero Down to Mono.

The screenshots below show the original mp3 (top) with left and right audio that appear to be just white noise, and the resulting mixed mono track (bottom).

There is a downside to this technique, however. Some online spectrogram viewing websites automatically mix the audio down to mono before rendering the spectrogram. Given players the answer without them even knowing that mixing was required.

2.1 // Second Iteration

To combat that issue, I took this technique to the extreme in chapter 4 of the RUNE ARG. Players are given 7 .wav files and told to find a message hidden in the noise. Each of the .wav files contains multiple sections of dense noise, sections of silence, and a single fetch command viewable in the spectrogram. All the immediately viewable commands are red herrings which do not help the play progress in the game.

The noise in these 7 track was carefully engineered so that a new message would be revealed only when all 7 were mixed together. When mixed, all the red herring messages get buried in dense noise, but a new message appears in the middle via destructive interference. This is the same principle as the XIXMAS puzzle, but with additional false leads, and it completely eliminates the pitfall of only spectrogram viewers. To my knowledge, there aren't any online tools that allow you to mix multiple files together. But even if there were, it would be a conscious choice of the player and not something they could skip without knowing.

3 // Physical Media

The trailhead for Puzzle 22 of the Find Buzzy ARG consisted of audio cassettes hidden in the Birmingham 8 theater in Birmingham, MI. The tapes were hidden in the hour leading up to a showing of the documentary Stolen Kingdom as part of the film's official Q&A Tour.

The cassettes contained the URL for the puzzle which could only be found by viewing the spectrogram of the audio. Unlike other techniques discussed on this page, there are no extra steps the player needs to take after viewing the spectrogram. Instead, we've made it more difficult to view the spectrogram by requiring the players to use a tape deck in order access the audio. While this medium does result in a loss of clarity in the spectrogram, it is still perfectly viable as shown in the screenshot below.

As additional note, the cassettes were packaged with custom labels and cases. While this aspect doesn't impact the puzzle itself, it does a lot to distinguish it from other spectrogram-based puzzles and certainly improves memorability.
